Learn Module · Introductory Activity

Faculty AI Readiness Inventory

An honest check-in on where you stand — not an evaluation, not a test. A starting point for thinking about AI in your teaching practice.

You don't need to be an AI expert to use it thoughtfully in your courses — or to talk about it credibly with your students. This inventory is designed to show you where you already have solid footing and where you might want to dig in deeper as you move through this course.

This isn't about your comfort level with technology. It's about your professional judgment, your pedagogical values, and what it means to teach in an AI-saturated world.

What to expect

This activity has four parts and takes about 20–25 minutes. You'll review a framework for thinking about AI in higher education, rate yourself on twelve pedagogical readiness statements, and write a short reflection that will inform how you engage with the rest of the course.

Part 1 of 3 · Context

The Landscape You're Teaching In

The U.S. Department of Labor's 2026 AI Literacy Framework describes what AI competency looks like for the workforce. It's also a useful map for thinking about what we're actually preparing students for — and what that demands of us as instructors.

1

Understand AI Principles

Knowing what AI actually is — how it works, what it can and can't do, and why those limitations matter in an academic context.

↳ Why faculty need conceptual fluency, not just tool fluency
2

Explore AI Uses

Understanding how students are already using AI — in your discipline, on your assignments, and often in ways you haven't anticipated.

↳ Mapping AI use cases across course types
3

Direct AI Effectively

Knowing enough about how prompting works that you can recognize good AI use from bad — and teach the difference to students.

↳ Prompting as a teachable skill
4

Evaluate AI Outputs

Being able to critically assess AI-generated content — for accuracy, voice, disciplinary appropriateness, and genuine intellectual contribution.

↳ Discernment as a learning outcome
5

Use AI Responsibly

Understanding the ethical, institutional, and pedagogical dimensions of AI — and being able to articulate a coherent position to your students, your department, and your institution.

↳ Policy, transparency, and academic integrity in the AI era
The question for faculty isn't just "should my students use AI?" It's "do I understand this well enough to make that call thoughtfully — and to help students use it in ways that actually serve their learning?"
